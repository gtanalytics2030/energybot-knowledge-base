The REC mechanism supplements fixed PPA path providing alternative revenue model with market upside.

Robust registration ensures only genuine, high-quality projects access REC markets.

**SUMMARY:** Sections 16.3-16.8 (condensed in this chunk for efficiency) establish comprehensive operational framework for REC projects covering: **(16.3) Generation Metering & Tracking:** REC projects deploy grid-standard metering (RTU/PMU per GERC standards) recording hourly generation data. REC projects provide RPO compliance pathway—obligated entities purchase RECs from projects or power exchanges. **(16.8) Revenue Certainty:** GERC-determined REC floor price ensures minimum revenue to project even if bilateral sales are weak, providing revenue floor while allowing upside in competitive markets. This framework enables project revenue diversification.

**PROVISION:** Section 17.1 - Energy Settlement Framework and Billing Cycle

**TITLE:** Energy Settlement and Banking: Power Exchange Framework and Billing Procedures

**SUMMARY:** Section 17.1 establishes the energy settlement framework—the accounting system tracking generation, consumption, and payments between generators, consumers, and DISCOMs. Settlement framework: (1) Settlement Entity: Energy settlement is administered by GUVNL (Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam Limited) acting as clearing house and nodal settlement agency; (2) Settlement Basis: Settlement calculates for each stakeholder: (a) Generated energy (from RE projects via metering), (b) Consumed energy (from consumers), (c) Transmission/distribution losses (calculated as percentage of throughput), (d) Net energy balance (generation minus consumption minus losses). (3) Billing Cycle: Settlement operates on 15-minute time-block basis (per Section 17.2) enabling granular energy accounting and real-time price discovery. Invoices are generated: (a) Daily: preliminary settlement for 99% of hours, (b) Monthly: final settlement after RTU data reconciliation and loss adjustments, (c) Annually: true-up settlement comparing actual losses to billed losses. (4) Tariff Components: Energy billing includes: (a) Energy charge (based on generation/consumption quantity × tariff rate), (b) Transmission charges (usage of transmission infrastructure per Section 18), (c) Reactive power charge (for lagging reactive power consumption), (d) Deviation settlement (payment/recovery for inadvertent variation from schedule per Section 17.4), (e) Net import tariff (for consuming state energy per Section 17.5). (5) Transparency: Detailed tariffs and formulas are published by GUVNL/GERC ensuring settlement calculations are auditable and traceable. Energy settlement's complexity requires sophisticated accounting ensuring fair burden-sharing among all participants.

**PROVISION:** Section 17.2 - 15-Minute Time-Block Settlement and Real-Time Energy Accounting

**TITLE:** Energy Settlement: Granular Time-Block Accounting and Pricing Precision

**SUMMARY:** Section 17.2 establishes 15-minute time-block settlement as the basis for granular energy accounting. Time-block mechanism: (1) Time-Block Definition: Day is divided into 96 x 15-minute blocks (96 blocks × 15 min = 24 hours). Each time-block is a distinct settlement unit with separate: (a) generation quantity, (b) consumption quantity, (c) schedule forecast (predicted generation/demand), (d) actual achievement vs. schedule (deviation), (e) pricing (block-specific energy rate reflecting demand/supply conditions). (2) Block-Specific Pricing: Energy rates vary by time-block reflecting: (a) Peak hours (6 AM-10 PM): higher demand → higher energy rates (e.g., ₹4-5/kWh), (b) Off-peak hours (10 PM-6 AM): lower demand → lower rates (e.g., ₹1-2/kWh), (c) Demand spikes: rates surge during grid stress (e.g., ₹8-10/kWh in shortage), (d) Supply abundance: rates fall when excess RE (e.g., midday solar): lower rates (₹0.5-1/kWh). (3) Real-Time Price Discovery: 15-minute blocks enable prices to reflect true supply-demand balance rather than averaged daily rate, improving grid efficiency; (4) Forecasting Alignment: Generators must forecast generation per 15-minute block (per Section 25.4), enabling SLDC to balance supply and demand in real-time; (5) Deviation Tracking: Actual generation vs. forecasted generation in each 15-minute block is tracked for inadvertent flow settlement (Section 17.4). Granular 15-minute settlement enables: (a) Demand-responsive energy pricing, (b) Grid stability through real-time balancing, (c) Transparent allocation of supply/demand imbalances. This mechanism aligns with All India Hour Ahead Market (AAM) framework adopted nationally.

**PROVISION:** Section 17.3 - Banking of Energy and Banking Charges

**TITLE:** Energy Banking: Energy Carryover Mechanism and Time-Shifted Banking Charges

**SUMMARY:** Section 17.3 establishes energy banking—enabling projects to offset generation variations across hours, days, or months via GUVNL's banking facility. Banking mechanism: (1) Energy Banking Concept: Project generates energy during high-resource periods (e.g., midday solar: noon-3 PM generating 40 MW) but consumer may only consume 30 MW. Excess 10 MW is "banked" in GUVNL's account as credit, redeemable when consumer needs energy (e.g., evening when solar unavailable). Banking converts intermittent generation into time-shifted consumption. (2) Banking Scope: Energy may be banked across: (a) Hourly banking: shifting generation 1-24 hours (e.g., midday solar → evening consumption), (b) Weekly banking: shifting generation across week (e.g., windy Wednesday → calm Saturday), (c) Monthly banking: shifting generation across month (e.g., March high wind → May low wind). (3) Banking Charges: Banked energy is charged: (a) Banking fee: percentage of banked energy (e.g., 3% of quantity) recovers GUVNL's administrative cost, transmission losses, and storage opportunity cost. Example: 100 MWh midday solar → 97 MWh credited for evening use (3 MWh = 3% loss); (b) Time-shifter cost: varies by time-shift duration—longer delays incur higher cost (e.g., 1-day shift = 2%, weekly shift = 5%, monthly shift = 10%). (4) Banking Limit: Banking typically limited to 25-30% of monthly generation preventing indefinite deferral; (5) Banking Period Expiry: Banked credits unused within banking window (typically 1 year) expire requiring redemption or forfeit. Banking enables intermittent projects (solar, wind) to match generation timing to consumption timing improving utilization.

**PROVISION:** Section 17.4 - Inadvertent Flow Settlement and Deviation Tariff

**TITLE:** Energy Settlement: Deviation Tariff and Inadvertent Energy Charge Mechanism

**SUMMARY:** Section 17.4 establishes settlement mechanism for "inadvertent flow"—unplanned energy flow from generator to distribution network when generation exceeds scheduled output or consumption. Inadvertent flow and deviation settlement: (1) Inadvertent Energy Definition: Occurs when: (a) Generator forecasts 50 MWh generation per 15-minute block but actually generates 55 MWh (5 MWh excess = inadvertent), or (b) Consumer forecasts 40 MWh consumption but uses only 35 MWh (5 MWh deficit = inadvertent), pushing surplus to grid. (2) Deviation Cause: Primarily from renewable intermittency—solar clouds reducing generation, wind gusts increasing generation, consumer load variability—creating mismatch between forecast and actual. (3) Grid Impact: Inadvertent flow destabilizes grid frequency (too much injection → frequency rise; too little → frequency drop). SLDC must continuously manage frequency preventing blackout. (4) Deviation Tariff: Generators/consumers paying for grid stabilization via deviation tariff charged per GERC orders. Two tariff rates: (a) Positive deviation (excess injection): generator pays penalty tariff (e.g., ₹0.50/kWh—cheaper than day-ahead to discourage overgeneration), (b) Negative deviation (deficit): generator pays shortfall tariff (e.g., ₹5/kWh—expensive to penalize underperformance). (5) Settlement: SLDC calculates daily: (a) Sum all generator forecasts vs. actuals, (b) Calculate each generator's deviation in each 15-min block, (c) Calculate deviation charge per generator, (d) Pool charges for collective SLDC cost recovery. (6) Relief Mechanism: Generators managing forecast accuracy (±5% error band typically excused) may be exempt from deviation charges, encouraging forecasting investment. Inadvertent flow mechanism incentivizes forecasting accuracy essential for grid stability.

**PROVISION:** Section 17.5 - Net Import Tariff and Power Procurement from Other States

**TITLE:** Energy Settlement: Net Import Tariff and Inter-State Power Procurement Mechanism

**SUMMARY:** Section 17.5 establishes net import tariff—the rate paid for energy sourced from outside Gujarat when local generation is insufficient to meet demand. Net import mechanism: (1) Net Import Definition: When Gujarat's total generation (own thermal + hydro + RE) is less than total consumption + transmission losses, the deficit energy is imported from neighboring states (Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka) or NRLDC pool. (2) Tariff Structure: Net import energy is charged at "net import tariff"—typically higher than local generation tariff reflecting: (a) Long-distance transmission costs, (b) Transmission losses (4-8% over interstate lines), (c) Seller state markup, (d) Time-of-supply variation (night imports more expensive than day). Example rates: Peak net import rate: ₹5.5-6/kWh (night); Off-peak net import rate: ₹3.5-4/kWh (midday). (3) Tariff Variation: Net import rate varies by: (a) Time-of-supply: peak hours costlier (night) vs. off-peak cheaper (day), (b) Season: summer peak rates > off-season, (c) Scarcity: when national shortage (drought, fuel crisis) import rates surge. (4) Strategic RE Expansion Role: Policy encourages RE expansion (solar, wind, BESS) to reduce net import reliance—each GW of local RE generation reduces net import requirement decreasing average power cost. This makes RE highly strategic: local generation at lower tariff > imported power at higher tariff. (5) Payment Mechanism: Net import energy is paid via GUVNL clearing house settlement with payments to seller states' DISCOMs. Net import tariff explicitly links local RE expansion to economic benefit (lower power cost) making 100 GW RE target economically rational.

**PROVISION:** Section 17.7 - Consumer-Level Banking and Rooftop Solar Banking

**TITLE:** Energy Banking: Distributed Consumer Banking and Net Metering Integration

**SUMMARY:** Section 17.7 addresses banking for distributed consumers—residential and commercial rooftop solar or micro-hydro systems. Consumer-level banking provisions: (1) Net Metering with Banking: Rooftop solar consumers generating excess energy feed it to distribution grid; via net metering, excess energy is: (a) credited to consumer account in GUVNL registry (0% loss rooftop to local transformer), (b) consumed by neighboring consumers via distribution grid, (c) credited back to original generator when grid supply is needed (reverse flow). (2) Consumer Banking Window: Rooftop solar energy may be banked and redeemed within: (a) Daily window (24-hour banking—midday solar → evening consumption), (b) Monthly window (entire month—seasonal consumption variation), (c) Quarterly window (3-month banking per distribution code provisions).
