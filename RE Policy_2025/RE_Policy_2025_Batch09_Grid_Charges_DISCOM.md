**PROVISION:** Section 18.1 - Wheeling Charges Framework and Transmission Access Cost

**TITLE:** Grid Charges: Wheeling Charge Structure for Third-Party Power Transmission

**SUMMARY:** Section 18.1 establishes wheeling charge framework—the cost for RE projects to use transmission and distribution infrastructure to transmit power from generation site to third-party consumers. Wheeling charges are determined by: (1) Regulatory Basis: GERC (Gujarat Electricity Regulatory Commission) issues annual Tariff Orders specifying wheeling charges per regulatory framework, typically aligned with CERC inter-state transmission methodology adapted to intra-state context; (2) Charge Components: Wheeling charge consists of: (a) Transmission Use of System (TUoS) charge—fixed cost for transmission line usage capacity reservation, typically ₹50-80 lakh/MVA/year for large transmission lines, (b) Variable transmission loss charge—percentage of energy transmitted (3-5% for intra-state transmission), (c) Distribution Use of System (DUoS) charge—for distribution network usage (₹0.50-1.50/kWh), (d) Distribution loss allowance—percentage loss recovery (5-8% for distribution). (3) Tariff Structure: Wheeling tariff is typically: (a) Fixed component—₹/kW/month for capacity reservation, (b) Variable component—₹/kWh for energy transmitted, (c) Example: Fixed ₹40/kW/month + Variable ₹0.75/kWh (transmission losses included). (4) Tariff Variation: Wheeling charges vary by: (a) Voltage level (110 kV, 33 kV, 11 kV—higher voltage = lower charge per unit), (b) Distance (longer transmission = higher charge), (c) Demand level (peak/off-peak differential charges). (5) Applicability: Wheeling charges apply to all RE projects using grid for third-party power transmission except: (a) Captive plants supplying own facility (exempt from wheeling), (b) DISCOM supply projects (different PPA-based tariff, no wheeling). Wheeling charges provide cost recovery for transmission infrastructure enabling open access.

**PROVISION:** Section 18.2 - Reactive Power Charges and Power Factor Requirements

**TITLE:** Grid Charges: Reactive Power Penalty and Power Factor Management

**SUMMARY:** Section 18.2 establishes reactive power charges—penalties for consuming inductive reactive power requiring additional grid infrastructure support. Reactive power mechanism: (1) Power Factor Definition: Electrical systems require both: (a) Real Power (active power in kW)—useful power doing work, (b) Reactive Power (VAR in kVAr)—non-productive power for maintaining magnetic fields. Power Factor = Real Power / Apparent Power = cosine(angle). Ideal power factor = 1.0 (100% real, 0% reactive); practical minimum = 0.95 lagging (95% real, 5% reactive). (2) Reactive Requirement: RE inverters and generation equipment naturally generate leading reactive power (beneficial to grid), but when loads consume lagging reactive power, grid must provide it via synchronous generators or STATCOM devices—expensive infrastructure. (3) Reactive Charge Mechanism: Consumers/generators exceeding permissible reactive power limit (e.g., >10% of real power) are charged per GERC tariff: (a) Excess reactive charge: ₹1-2/kVAr/month typical (charged on excess VAR beyond permissible limit), (b) Cumulative penalties: large consumers may owe ₹50-500 lakh/month if severely non-compliant; (c) Relief: installing power factor correction capacitors reduces reactive charge. (4) RE Project Benefit: Modern RE inverters have advanced controls enabling: (a) Leading reactive power injection (supporting grid voltage), (b) Zero reactive consumption (unity power factor operation), (c) Reactive support for grid stability—enabling 5-10% charge reduction. (5) Applicability: Reactive charges apply to: (a) Large industrial consumers >1 MW, (b) Third-party wheeling (consumers), (c) Exemption: Small rooftop solar consumers (<10 kW) typically exempt. Reactive power charges incentivize power factor improvement reducing grid strain.

**PROVISION:** Section 18.3 - Cross-Subsidy Surcharge and Distribution Cost Recovery

**TITLE:** Grid Charges: Cross-Subsidy Surcharge for Non-DISCOM Sales

**SUMMARY:** Section 18.3 establishes cross-subsidy surcharge—an additional charge on open-access consumers to recover distribution cost that would have been recovered from DISCOM sales. Cross-subsidy surcharge mechanism: (1) Policy Rationale: DISCOMs serve diverse consumer base with: (a) Profitable HT industrial consumers (large demand, high tariff), (b) Subsidized LT agricultural consumers (high volume, low tariff per government policy), (c) Domestic consumers (moderate tariff). DISCOM tariff averaging across all categories ensures cross-subsidy: profitable industrial offsetting agricultural subsidy. When industrial consumer switches to open-access via wheeling, that profit is lost, and agricultural subsidy burden falls on remaining consumers. (2) Surcharge Calculation: Cross-subsidy surcharge = (DISCOM average tariff - Market price) × wheeled energy quantity. Example: DISCOM average tariff ₹6/kWh, market spot price ₹3/kWh, cross-subsidy = ₹3/kWh. If wheeling 10 MWh, surcharge = ₹30 lakh. (3) Surcharge Structure per GERC: Typical cross-subsidy rates vary: (a) HT consumers (>100 kW): ₹0.50-1.50/kWh, (b) Industrial consumers: ₹1-2/kWh, (c) Agricultural consumers exempt, (d) Domestic consumers exempt. (4) Purpose: Surcharge compensates DISCOM for distribution cost allocation—ensuring agricultural subsidy continues and DISCOMs remain financially viable despite open-access competition. (5) Policy Perspective: Cross-subsidy surcharge moderates open-access expansion (making wheeling costlier) while protecting agricultural tariff subsidies. Over time, increasing agricultural cost burden may drive policy toward direct subsidy (fiscal payment) rather than cross-subsidy (tariff cross-recovery). Cross-subsidy surcharge is contentious—industrial users view it as anti-competitive; DISCOMs view it as necessary for financial sustainability.

**PROVISION:** Section 18.4 - Multi-Location Surcharge and Distributed Supply Premium

**TITLE:** Grid Charges: Additional Surcharge for Multi-Location Power Supply

**SUMMARY:** Section 18.4 establishes multi-location surcharge—an additional charge when a single RE project supplies multiple consumer locations (distributed supply) via common transmission infrastructure. Multi-location surcharge mechanism: (1) Definition: Multi-location supply occurs when: (a) Single RE project (e.g., 10 MW solar farm) supplies: (i) Textile unit's headquarters (5 MW), (ii) Textile unit's satellite plant 50 km away (3 MW), (iii) Textile unit's port facility 100 km away (2 MW). Rather than 3 separate direct lines (expensive), single project line splits at distribution substations sending power to three locations. (2) Surcharge Justification: Multi-location supply increases transmission cost via: (a) Longer distribution distance (100+ km for distant locations), (b) Additional transformer/substation connections, (c) Increased distribution losses (longer circuits), (d) Enhanced voltage regulation need (remote locations prone to voltage drop). (3) Surcharge Rate: Per GERC, multi-location surcharge: (a) 5 paise/kWh (₹0.05/kWh) for supply within 10 km of primary location, (b) 10 paise/kWh (₹0.10/kWh) for supply 10-50 km away, (c) 20 paise/kWh (₹0.20/kWh) for supply >50 km away. Cumulative: supplying 3 locations 100 km away = 20 paise/kWh surcharge on total supply. (4) Alternative: Instead of surcharge, consumer may: (a) Setup independent dedicated line (more costly), (b) Accept wheeling surcharge instead of multi-location surcharge (lesser of two). (5) Policy Perspective: Multi-location surcharge discourages distributed consumer supply (preferring concentrated supply) while revenue incentivizes DISCOMs for long-distance supply support. This surcharge is emerging mechanism as captive supply networks expand geographically.

**TITLE:** Grid Charges: Miscellaneous Infrastructure Surcharge and System Improvement Contribution

**SUMMARY:** Section 18.5 establishes additional surcharge (also termed "grid improvement surcharge" or "system maintenance fee") to fund grid infrastructure improvement, cybersecurity hardening, and SLDC operational enhancement. Additional surcharge mechanism: (1) Purpose: Open-access users and RE projects utilize grid infrastructure benefiting from: (a) Transmission line maintenance and upgrade, (b) Substation automation and control systems, (c) SLDC cybersecurity and digital resilience, (d) RTU/PMU installation and communication, (e) System studies and planning. This incentivizes off-peak consumption shifting. (5) Mandatory Imposition: Additional surcharge is typically mandatory on all open-access consumers and RE wheeling participants—no exemption for small consumers unlike cross-subsidy (agricultural exempt). Additional surcharge is politically less contentious than cross-subsidy as it directly correlates to infrastructure investment.

**TITLE:** DISCOM Power Purchase: Competitive Procurement and Tariff Determination

**SUMMARY:** Section 19.1 establishes DISCOM (Distribution Licensee) power purchase framework for procuring RE energy to meet demand and RPO obligations. DISCOM procurement mechanism: (1) Procurement Authority: Distribution Licensees (DISCOMs: DGVCL, MGVCL, PGVCL, UGVCL for distribution; TPL, Surat, Dahej, AIVPL, Dholera for dedicated/private) procure RE via competitive bidding or direct contracts per GERC guidelines. Process: (a) DISCOM sets bid target (e.g., 500 MW), (b) Bidders submit technical proposal + tariff bid (₹/kWh), (c) Technical evaluation—ALMM, resource assessment, financial capacity check, (d) Tariff bid opening—lowest tariff selected (assuming technicality compliant), (e) Winner executed PPA. Diversified procurement reduces resource and technology concentration risk. (5) Multi-Year Contracting: DISCOMs typically execute long-term contracts: (a) 25-year PPAs (common for solar/wind), (b) 10-year fixed price period + 5-year escalation clause (annual 2-3% increase), (c) Capacity factor guarantee—if below contracted level, tariff adjustment provided. (6) Strategic Value: RE procurement by DISCOMs is strategic: (a) Reduces power purchase cost (RE tariff lower than thermal ₹5-7/kWh), (b) Meets RPO obligations (mandatory 17-20% renewable by 2030), (c) Reduces GHG emissions (125 grams CO2/kWh avoided vs. thermal), (d) Improves portfolio stability (long-term price certainty). Competitive bidding drives innovation and cost reduction benefiting consumers.

(5) Time-of-Supply Incentives: Policy incentivizes: (a) Peak renewable supply (solar during peak afternoon): 1% additional RPO credit, (b) Firm renewable (BESS-backed generation): 2% additional RPO credit—encouraging reliability. These incentives favor RE when grid needs it most. (6) Portfolio Approach: DISCOMs develop RE portfolio balancing: (a) Cost (cheaper tariff), (b) Reliability (firm capacity + intermittent), (c) Temporal matching (peak solar + evening wind), (d) Geography (distributed + centralized). Exemption framework: (1) **Solar Exemption:** Solar projects ≤4 MW capacity may be procured directly (non-competitive) by DISCOMs at: (a) Pre-fixed tariff per GERC (e.g., ₹2.75/kWh for 4 MW solar, FY 2025 benchmark), (b) Streamlined approval (30-day contract execution vs. 90+ days for bidding), (c) Suitable for: (i) Rooftop solar on DISCOM office/substation buildings, (ii) Small ground-mounted solar on wasteland, (iii) Distributed solar in peri-urban areas. (2) **Wind Exemption:** Wind projects ≤10 MW capacity may be procured directly at: (a) Pre-fixed tariff per GERC (e.g., ₹3.10/kWh for wind 10 MW, FY 2025), (b) Rationale: Wind projects typically ≥50 MW minimum (optimal turbine size), hence ≤10 MW is small/pilot; exemption supports small wind development, (c) Suitable for: (i) Repowering projects 5-10 MW (Section 15.5), (ii) Wind-diesel hybrid in remote areas, (iii) Pilot demonstration projects. (3) **Purpose of Exemption:** Small project exemption: (a) Reduces transaction cost for small developers, (b) Accelerates deployment for small projects (major approval bottleneck in bidding), (c) Encourages distributed/decentralized RE (supporting green jobs in multiple locations), (d) Avoids adverse selection (small projects unlikely to win competitive bidding). (4) **Tariff Setting:** Exempted project tariffs are: (a) Set by GERC once per year (e.g., FY 2025 ≠ FY 2024), (b) Cost-based—reflecting technology cost + reasonable return (8-10%), (c) Typically 10-15% higher than competitive bids (reflecting higher unit cost for small projects), (d) Published in advance enabling developers to assess project viability. (5) **Combined Procurement:** DISCOM typically procures: (a) Large projects via competitive bidding (80-90% of capacity), (b) Small exempted projects via direct contract (10-20% of capacity), (c) Example: DGVCL 1000 MW solar target = 850 MW competitive bidding + 150 MW exempted ≤4 MW projects. This combined approach balances cost efficiency (large/competitive) with deployment speed (small/exempted). (6) **Exemption Cap:** Individual DISCOM exempted capacity is typically capped: (a) ≤4 MW solar: 30% of annual solar target, (b) ≤10 MW wind: 25% of annual wind target. Caps prevent excessive non-competitive procurement. Exemptions are pro-developer tools accelerating small project deployment.

**SUMMARY:** Section 19.4 establishes tariff averaging mechanism and price stability provisions for small exempted projects. Tariff averaging framework: (1) **Definition:** Rather than setting separate tariff for each exempted project (which would be administratively burdensome), GERC determines: (a) Single "average tariff" for all projects of same technology and capacity band, (b) Example: All solar projects 3-4 MW approved in FY 2025 get same tariff ₹2.75/kWh regardless of location or developer, (c) Applied across all DISCOMs uniformly (DGVCL, MGVCL, PGVCL, UGVCL all same tariff). (2) **Six-Month Fixed Window:** Tariff remains fixed for 6-month period from GERC publication: (a) Publication in April 2025: solar ≤4 MW tariff ₹2.75/kWh, (b) Fixed from April 2025 - September 2025 (6 months), (c) Next tariff revision: October 2025 (new 6-month window October 2025 - March 2026), (d) Developers have 6-month certainty to prepare bids/contracts based on published tariff. (3) **Tariff Revision Frequency:** GERC revises exempted tariffs: (a) Semi-annually (twice per year—April and October) reflecting: (i) Equipment cost changes (panel/turbine price), (ii) Interest rate changes (financing cost), (iii) Inflation/deflation of input costs.
